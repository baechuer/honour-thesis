\chapter*{Abstract}

Large language model agents increasingly rely on reusable skills that package instructions, workflows, resources, dependencies, and constraints. The core difficulty is selecting the correct skill when several share a topic but differ in inputs, outputs, procedures, resources, or authority boundaries.

This thesis asks which operational information distinguishes semantically similar but procedurally distinct skills, and how representation and retrieval choices preserve or use it. Candidate fields are motivated by the literature and by a full-document binary review of 29,292 public skills, which finds every candidate type common but not universal. RQ1 combines 350 controlled three-sibling clusters with source-grounded information removal from 1,099 public originals. RQ2a holds queries, candidates, and operational propositions fixed while comparing matched fielded, flat, prose, order-controlled, and diluted representations. RQ2b compares native metadata, complete bodies, unlabelled evidence, and field-labelled evidence across a 2,433-skill library and 381 strict-gold prompts.

All seven RQ1 fields resolve deliberately tied near-neighbours in the controlled setting, although their robustness varies by prompt and retriever. Examples/tests are a negative control: generic examples support execution without defining distinct capabilities. In public originals, removing use-condition or success/verification evidence lowers strict Hit@1 under both BM25 and Qwen; other individual fields are retriever-conditional. RQ2a shows that restoring candidate-specific operational facts improves every selector over a shared-only control, whereas explicit headings are not universally beneficial: versus matched flat text, they improve BM25 top-1 by 2.1 points, reduce Qwen single-vector top-1 by 6.7 points, and change SkillRouter cross-encoder top-1 by -0.4 points.

On RQ2b's public-gold stratum, complete bodies exceed field-labelled evidence in first-stage top-1 for BM25 (0.644 versus 0.504), Qwen (0.686 versus 0.538), and SkillRouter (0.806 versus 0.746), while field-labelled evidence reduces selector-visible corpus tokens by 72--76\% and document/index time by 64--79\%. The thesis therefore supports an accuracy--compression trade-off, not a universal advantage for field labels or a claim that field presence alone improves retrieval.
