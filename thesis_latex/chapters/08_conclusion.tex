\chapter{Conclusion}

This thesis investigates operational information and representation strategies for scalable agent skill retrieval. The motivating problem is semantic confusion: as skill libraries grow, many skills can sound relevant while requiring different inputs, outputs, workflows, dependencies, or boundaries. In these cases, the correct skill may exist in the library, but the selector may surface a plausible yet procedurally inappropriate alternative.

The thesis uses a layered vocabulary for skill artifacts, skill representations, and skill retrieval. This vocabulary is useful because a full skill artifact may contain rich procedural information, while the selector may see only a compressed card. Conversely, simply exposing more text does not guarantee better retrieval if the relevant signal is diluted.

The earlier frozen-v0.4 benchmark contains 2433 skills, 245 controlled prompts, 144 public-gold prompts, and 12 low-information stress prompts. Its representation, SkillRouter, and M6 matrices remain exploratory evidence that representation and selector choice interact. The thesis-facing full-library result instead uses the later source-grounded V3 strict-gold endpoint: 2,433 skills and 381 prompts, comprising 243 controlled and 138 public-gold prompts.

The exploratory evidence also shows that lexical baselines are strong. This prevents the thesis from being framed as a simple dense-embedding-versus-structure comparison; the full-library result similarly requires each representation to be compared under the same first-stage retriever and candidate set.

The controlled answer to RQ1 is that all seven tested operational information types can help distinguish near-neighbour skills when the candidate-specific field is visible. The result is not a ranking: each field uses a different 50-cluster suite, and robustness changes with prompt wording and retriever. Input requirements remain robust under paraphrase, Qwen handles paraphrased output requirements better than BM25, and implicit boundaries, paraphrased success criteria, and fine-grained workflows are harder for both. Dependency information is useful when it expresses concrete capability compatibility rather than generic setup commands. Examples/tests provide a negative control: generic examples do not reliably distinguish capabilities, although near-exact request/example matches can strongly attract a selector.

The field set was not selected from the controlled benchmark alone. A separate model-assisted binary review of 29,292 frozen public primary skill documents found each candidate type in 64.91--83.34\% of complete documents, with use condition in 80.68\%, output/artifact in 79.76\%, workflow/procedure in 83.34\%, and dependency/resource in 75.32\%. This full-document census includes native names and descriptions as well as Markdown content, and therefore supports only the descriptive claim that these information types are common in the sampled public skill ecosystem. Together with the literature review, it provides the basis for proposing the RQ1 fields; the controlled and source-removal experiments, rather than this prevalence count, establish their routing relevance.

The public original-document experiment qualifies this answer. In the purposefully selected, removal-eligible public groups, removing cited use-condition and success/verification information lowers strict Hit@1 under both BM25 and Qwen, while input, output, workflow, and dependency effects are stable only for BM25; boundary/not-for has no stable positive Top-1 effect. Group removal of task-specification or execution/verification information lowers Hit@1 for both retrievers. These groups therefore contain both useful and redundant evidence. The edit may leave a skill incomplete, so this result concerns routing after information removal rather than execution. All controlled clusters, public gold labels, and scored removals were confirmed by the researcher with the labels or edits visible; this was not independent or blinded annotation.

RQ2a now has a confirmatory answer. Across 280 untouched clusters, 600 prompts, 22 conditions, and 13,200 aligned fixed-candidate decisions, operational facts are essential but field headings are not universally beneficial. Relative to matched flat propositions, fielded text improves BM25 by 2.1 percentage points, reduces Qwen single-vector top-1 by 6.7 points, and changes SkillRouter cross-encoder top-1 by only -0.4 points. Frozen Qwen field-aware scoring recovers 6.6 points over fielded single-vector selection, but its 0.823 top-1 is essentially equal to Qwen flat at 0.824. SkillRouter is the strongest selector, reaching 0.955 on flat text, but its joint query--candidate cross-encoder inference is substantially more expensive than cached bi-encoder or lexical scoring.

The bounded answer to RQ2a is therefore that representation value depends on retrieval architecture and field type. Preserving operational facts matters consistently; merely displaying headings does not. Explicit field-wise scoring is valuable for some conditional fields and can repair pooled-vector loss, but the frozen uniform-top-two rule is not a universal replacement for a strong flat representation.

RQ2b compares I1 native metadata, I2 original full bodies, I3-flat evidence, and field-labelled I3C under three first-stage retrievers and two fixed-Top-20 rerankers. It finds an accuracy--compression trade-off: on public-gold B1, I2 exceeds I3C under BM25 (0.644 versus 0.504), Qwen (0.686 versus 0.538), and SkillRouter (0.806 versus 0.746), while I3C reduces selector-visible corpus tokens by 72--76\% and measured document/index time by 64--79\%. I3C also remains below I2 in five of six public fixed-list reranking configurations. No representation universally dominates.

Only 4 of 755 retained I3 items associated with strict-gold prompt records are description-visible. Literal duplication therefore does not explain I1's competitive performance, while other causal explanations remain untested. The strict-label endpoint and provenance/availability checks do not establish unique task correctness, field-level causality, independently reviewed semantic completeness of I3C, or complete construction cost.

The contribution is not a universal skill-router benchmark or a provider leaderboard. It is a field-isolated benchmark and information-centred empirical account of why semantically similar skills are difficult to retrieve, which operational information appears to matter, and how representation and retrieval strategies can preserve, organise, exploit, or fail to use that information. RQ2a establishes the controlled mechanism result; RQ2b establishes the full-library accuracy--compression trade-off under source-grounded strict-label evaluation. Representation and retrieval must therefore be evaluated together rather than ranked as independent ingredients. Tree/graph retrieval and downstream execution remain future work.
